## Skills by group (id — title: description)

### algebraic-manipulation
- rewrite-using-index-laws — Rewrite expressions using index laws: Turn roots, reciprocals and products into powers of x (e.g. 1/sqrt(x) = x^(-1/2)) or into a common base before differentiating, integrating or solving.
- simplify-algebraic-fractions — Combine and simplify algebraic fractions: Put fractions over a common denominator, multiply through by denominators and cancel common factors.
- rearrange-to-make-subject — Rearrange a formula to change its subject: Collect the terms containing the required letter, factorise it out and divide, keeping the order of operations correct.
- solve-simultaneous-linear-equations — Solve two linear simultaneous equations: Form two equations from two pieces of information and solve them together to find two unknown constants.
- exact-surd-manipulation — Simplify surds and rationalise denominators: Write answers as simplified surds (e.g. 3sqrt(2)) and rationalise denominators where required.
- solve-power-equation — Solve an equation of the form x^n = k: Isolate the power (e.g. r^3 = 1050, p^7 = 1.5625, r^(3/2) = 118) and take the appropriate root or power.
- substitute-to-find-intersections — Substitute to find intersections: Substitute one equation into another (a line into a curve or circle, or parametric x(t), y(t) into a Cartesian equation) to get a single equation to solve.

### quadratics
- solve-quadratic-equation — Solve a quadratic equation: Solve a three-term quadratic by factorising, the quadratic formula or completing the square, giving exact roots when asked.
- complete-the-square — Complete the square: Write ax^2 + bx + c as a(x + p)^2 + q to read off turning points, show an expression is always positive, or find a circle centre.
- discriminant-condition — Use the discriminant b^2 - 4ac: Choose the right condition (> 0 two roots, = 0 tangent/repeated root, < 0 no real roots) and solve for the unknown constant.
- solve-quadratic-inequality — Solve a quadratic (or other) inequality: Find the critical values, then use a sketch or sign analysis to choose the inside or outside region.
- solve-hidden-quadratic — Spot and solve a quadratic in disguise: Recognise a quadratic in e^x, 2^x, r^5, y^2 or similar, substitute u for it, solve, then convert back and reject impossible values.

### polynomials
- factor-theorem — Use the factor theorem: If (x - a) is a factor then f(a) = 0: substitute to find an unknown constant or to confirm a factor.
- factorise-polynomial-by-division — Factorise a cubic using a known factor: Divide (by long division or inspection) by a known linear factor to get a quadratic factor, then solve or analyse it.
- find-polynomial-from-features — Find a quadratic or cubic from its features: Use roots, repeated roots, a turning point, an intercept or given points (from a graph or a context) to find the equation, e.g. y = ax(x - 6)^2, H = ax^2 + bx + c.

### graphs-and-transformations
- graph-transformations — Apply graph transformations: Map points or curves under y = af(x), f(x + a), f(ax), -f(x), f(-x) and combinations, or describe a transformation fully.
- sketch-curve-key-features — Sketch a curve showing key features: Draw the correct shape (including exponential, modulus and polynomial curves) and label intercepts, turning points, asymptotes and end behaviour.
- modulus-graph-vertex — Sketch modulus graphs and find the vertex: Sketch y = a|x - b| + c or y = |f(x)| and read off the vertex and intercepts.
- solve-modulus-equation-or-inequality — Solve modulus equations and inequalities: Remove the modulus by taking the relevant branch(es) (or cases), solve, and check each solution against the graph.
- line-meets-graph-critical-cases — Find the values of k for which a line meets a graph: Use the graph to find critical cases (through a vertex or endpoint, parallel to a branch, tangent) and combine them into a range of k.
- graphical-root-counting — Use a graph to justify the number of roots: Rearrange an equation into two graphs you can sketch and count the intersections to explain how many roots there are.

### functions
- composite-functions — Evaluate and form composite functions: Work out fg(x) by applying g first and then f (choosing the right branch of a piecewise function); evaluate at a value or simplify fully.
- inverse-functions — Find and use inverse functions: Swap x and y and rearrange to find f^(-1)(x), state its domain from the range of f, and know when an inverse exists (one-one).
- domain-and-range — State domains and ranges: Use the graph, turning point or asymptote of a function to state its range (or the domain of its inverse) with correct notation.

### straight-lines-coordinates
- equation-of-straight-line — Find the equation of a straight line: Use y - y1 = m(x - x1) with a gradient and a point, and give the answer in the form asked for.
- perpendicular-gradients — Use perpendicular gradients: A perpendicular line (a normal, or a radius meeting a tangent) has gradient equal to the negative reciprocal: m1 x m2 = -1.
- distance-between-points — Find distances between points: Use Pythagoras on the coordinate differences to find a length or a distance between centres.

### circles
- circle-centre-and-radius — Find a circle's centre and radius (or write its equation): Complete the square on x and y to get (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 = r^2 and read off the centre and radius, or write the equation from a known centre and radius.
- two-circles-and-distance-to-circle — Solve problems with two circles or distances to a circle: Use the distance between centres (and the radii) to decide how circles intersect, or find the furthest/nearest point.

### sequences-and-series
- arithmetic-sequence-nth-term — Use the nth term of an arithmetic sequence: Use u_n = a + (n - 1)d to find a term, the common difference or the number of terms.
- arithmetic-series-sum — Sum an arithmetic series: Use S_n = n/2[2a + (n - 1)d] (or n/2(a + l)), often setting it equal to a total to form a quadratic in n.
- geometric-sequence-nth-term — Use the nth term of a geometric sequence: Use u_n = ar^(n - 1) to find a term or the common ratio, including percentage-growth contexts.
- geometric-series-sum — Sum a finite geometric series: Use S_n = a(1 - r^n)/(1 - r) with the correct first term and number of terms.
- sum-to-infinity-and-convergence — Use the sum to infinity and the convergence condition: A geometric series converges only if |r| < 1; then S_infinity = a/(1 - r).
- geometric-ratio-equation — Form an equation using the common ratio: For three consecutive terms, set term2/term1 = term3/term2 and simplify to an equation in the unknown.
- prove-series-sum-formula — Prove the arithmetic or geometric series formula: Write S_n forwards and backwards and add (arithmetic), or multiply by r and subtract (geometric).
- recurrence-relation-terms — Generate terms from a recurrence relation: Substitute repeatedly into u_(n+1) = f(u_n) to find terms, often in terms of an unknown constant, and form an equation.
- periodic-sequence-sum — Sum a periodic sequence: Identify the repeating block and its order, then sum complete blocks plus any leftover terms.
- sigma-notation-sums — Evaluate sums in sigma notation: Split a sigma sum into standard parts, adjust for a starting index other than 1, or spot a telescoping pattern.

### binomial-expansion
- binomial-take-out-factor — Take out a factor before expanding (a + bx)^n: Rewrite (a + bx)^n as a^n(1 + bx/a)^n before using the expansion.
- binomial-validity — State the range of validity of an expansion: The expansion of (1 + ax)^n is valid for |ax| < 1; use this to decide which x values may be substituted.
- binomial-approximation — Use an expansion to approximate a value: Choose a suitable x, substitute into both the expression and the expansion, and comment on accuracy or over/underestimate.
- multiply-series-and-truncate — Multiply series and truncate at a power: Multiply two expansions (or an expansion by a polynomial) keeping only terms up to the required power.
- binomial-expansion-terms — Write out binomial expansion terms: Expand (1 + ax)^n for fractional or negative n (1 + nx + n(n-1)/2! (ax)^2 + ...), or find a coefficient in (a + bx)^n with nCr, bracketing (ax)^k correctly.

### exponentials-and-logarithms
- laws-of-logarithms — Apply the laws of logarithms: Use the addition, subtraction and power laws (and log_a a = 1) to combine or split logarithms.
- solve-exponential-equation — Solve exponential equations using logarithms: Isolate the exponential term (or set two exponential models equal), then take logs to bring the power down and solve.
- remove-logarithms — Convert a log equation into an equation without logs: Combine into a single log, then use log_a x = b <=> x = a^b (or e^(ln x) = x) to remove the logs.

### exponential-modelling
- form-exponential-model — Find the constants in an exponential model: Substitute given data (usually t = 0 first) into V = Ae^(kt), V = Ap^t or theta = A - Be^(-kt) and solve for the constants.
- log-linear-graph-model — Use a straight-line log graph to find model constants: Take logs of y = ab^x or y = kx^n to get a straight line; match the gradient and intercept to find the constants.
- rate-of-change-of-model — Differentiate a model to find a rate of change: Differentiate the model with respect to time and substitute a value, or use a given rate to find a constant.

### modelling-in-context
- translate-context-into-model — Translate a context into a mathematical model: Turn a worded description into an equation: linear cost, quadratic path, arithmetic/geometric growth, or a proportional rate.
- use-model-to-predict — Use a model to make a prediction: Substitute a value into a model (or solve for the input), including an optimum found by calculus, and answer in context with units.
- evaluate-model-against-data — Evaluate a model against real data: Compare a model prediction with a given true value and conclude whether the model is reliable, with a reason.
- interpret-model-parameters — Interpret constants in a model: Explain what a constant means in context (initial value, growth factor per year, cost per item).
- state-model-limitation — State a limitation of a model: Give a specific reason the model may break down (negative values, unrealistic assumptions, outside the data range).
- long-term-behaviour-of-model — Find the long-term (limiting) value of a model: Let t tend to infinity (e^(-kt) -> 0) or set the rate to zero to find a limiting value, and interpret it.

### radians-and-circular-measure
- arc-length — Find arc lengths (radians): Use s = r theta with theta in radians, including perimeters of sectors and composite shapes.
- sector-area — Find sector areas (radians): Use A = (1/2) r^2 theta with theta in radians.
- composite-circular-regions — Find areas and perimeters of composite circular regions: Build a region from sectors, triangles and segments (e.g. overlapping circles, logos), adding and subtracting pieces.
- triangle-trigonometry — Use sine rule, cosine rule and (1/2)ab sin C: Find sides, angles or areas of non-right-angled triangles.
- exact-trig-values — Use exact trig values: Use exact values of sin, cos and tan at 0, pi/6, pi/4, pi/3, pi/2 (and related angles) to keep answers exact.
- small-angle-approximations — Use small angle approximations: Replace sin x ~ x, tan x ~ x and cos x ~ 1 - x^2/2 (x in radians, watch the argument, e.g. cos 3x ~ 1 - 9x^2/2).

### trig-identities
- reciprocal-trig-functions — Use sec, cosec and cot: Rewrite sec, cosec and cot as 1/cos, 1/sin and cos/sin (and tan as sin/cos) to simplify.
- pythagorean-identities — Use Pythagorean identities: Use sin^2 + cos^2 = 1, 1 + tan^2 = sec^2 and 1 + cot^2 = cosec^2 to change the form of an expression.
- double-angle-formulae — Use double angle formulae: Use sin 2A = 2 sin A cos A and the three forms of cos 2A, choosing the form that simplifies the expression.
- compound-angle-formulae — Use compound angle formulae: Expand sin(A +- B), cos(A +- B), tan(A +- B), e.g. to find cos 3A or to rewrite a shifted trig function.
- prove-trig-identity — Prove a trigonometric identity: Start from one side, convert to sin and cos, use identities and algebra step by step until you reach the other side.
- harmonic-form — Write a cos x + b sin x as R cos(x +- alpha) or R sin(x +- alpha): Find R = sqrt(a^2 + b^2) and alpha from tan alpha, matching the signs to the required form.

### trig-equations-and-models
- solve-trig-equation-in-interval — Solve a trig equation in a given interval: Find the principal value, then use the symmetry/period of the graph to list every solution in the interval (degrees or radians).
- solve-trig-with-transformed-argument — Solve trig equations with a transformed argument: For sin(2x + 30) = k etc., adjust the interval for the new argument, solve, then undo the transformation.
- solve-trig-quadratic — Solve a quadratic in a trig function: Use identities to get a quadratic in one trig function, solve it, and reject values outside [-1, 1].
- factorise-not-divide-trig — Factorise rather than divide to avoid losing solutions: When a common factor such as sin x or sin 2x appears, factorise it out and solve each factor, instead of cancelling it.
- trig-model-max-min-and-times — Use a trig model to find maximum, minimum and times: Use the maximum/minimum of sin or cos (+-1, or R for harmonic form) to find extreme values and the times they occur in context.
- trig-model-parameters — Find the parameters of a trig model: Use the maximum, minimum, period and starting value to find the constants in a sinusoidal model.

### basic-differentiation
- differentiate-polynomials — Differentiate polynomials: Use d/dx(x^n) = nx^(n-1) term by term on polynomials.
- differentiate-negative-and-fractional-powers — Differentiate negative and fractional powers: Rewrite roots and reciprocals as powers, then use nx^(n-1) carefully with negative and fractional indices.
- differentiate-exponentials-and-logs — Differentiate exponentials and logarithms: Use d/dx(e^(kx)) = ke^(kx) and d/dx(ln x) = 1/x (and ln(f(x)) -> f'(x)/f(x)).
- differentiate-trig-functions — Differentiate trig functions: Differentiate sin(kx), cos(kx), tan(kx) and the reciprocal trig functions, including with the chain rule.
- second-derivative — Find the second derivative: Differentiate the first derivative again (including quotient-rule second derivatives).
- differentiate-from-first-principles — Differentiate from first principles: Write the gradient of the chord (f(x + h) - f(x))/h, simplify, and take the limit as h -> 0 with correct notation.

### chain-product-quotient-rules
- chain-rule — Differentiate using the chain rule: Differentiate a function of a function: d/dx f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) g'(x), e.g. (3 + 7e^(-0.25t))^(-1).
- product-rule — Differentiate using the product rule: Use d/dx(uv) = u v' + v u' for products such as x ln x or e^(-2x)(x^2 - 2).
- quotient-rule — Differentiate using the quotient rule: Use d/dx(u/v) = (v u' - u v')/v^2, then simplify the numerator.
- simplify-derivative-to-given-form — Simplify a derivative to a given form: Factorise and cancel common factors (e.g. e^(-2x), (x + 1)) so the derivative matches the printed form exactly.

### implicit-and-parametric-differentiation
- implicit-differentiation — Differentiate implicitly: Differentiate each term with respect to x, using y^n -> ny^(n-1) dy/dx and the product rule on xy terms, then make dy/dx the subject.
- parametric-differentiation — Differentiate parametric equations: Use dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt), then substitute the parameter value for the point.
- find-parameter-at-point — Find the parameter value at a given point: Solve x(t) = a or y(t) = b (checking the other coordinate) to find the parameter at a point, then use it.
- connected-rates-of-change — Link rates of change with the chain rule: Combine rates such as dV/dt = (dV/dr)(dr/dt) or dh/dt = (dh/dV)(dV/dt) using a volume formula.

### tangents-and-normals
- tangent-at-a-point — Find the equation of a tangent: Differentiate, substitute the x-coordinate for the gradient, and use y - y1 = m(x - x1).
- normal-at-a-point — Find the equation of a normal: Find the tangent gradient, take the negative reciprocal, and use the point to write the normal.
- gradient-conditions-on-curves — Use gradient conditions (horizontal/vertical tangents, equal gradients): Interpret dy/dx = 0, dy/dx undefined, or f'(x) = g'(x) geometrically to set up the equation to solve.

### stationary-points
- set-derivative-zero-and-solve — Set the derivative equal to zero and solve: Stationary points occur where dy/dx = 0: set the derivative to zero, solve, and find the coordinates.
- verify-stationary-point — Verify a stationary point at a given x: Substitute the given x into dy/dx, show it equals 0 and make a conclusion.
- second-derivative-test — Determine the nature of a stationary point: Substitute into the second derivative (positive: minimum, negative: maximum) or check the gradient either side (needed for a stationary point of inflection); state the sign and conclude.
- increasing-decreasing-functions — Use the derivative to find increasing/decreasing intervals: f is increasing where f'(x) > 0 and decreasing where f'(x) < 0; solve the inequality or prove the sign.
- concavity-and-inflection — Use concavity and points of inflection: Concave where f''(x) <= 0, convex where f''(x) >= 0; at a point of inflection f''(x) changes sign.

### optimisation
- eliminate-variable-using-constraint — Eliminate a variable using a constraint: Use the fixed quantity (e.g. volume) to express one variable in terms of the other and substitute into the quantity to be optimised.
- mensuration-formulae — Use area and volume formulae for 3D shapes: Write surface areas and volumes of prisms, cylinders, spheres/hemispheres and cuboids.

### basic-integration
- integrate-powers-of-x — Integrate powers of x: Use the integral of x^n = x^(n+1)/(n+1) term by term, including negative and fractional powers after rewriting.
- evaluate-definite-integral — Evaluate a definite integral: Substitute the limits into the integrated expression and subtract (upper minus lower), simplifying exactly; recognise lim (delta x -> 0) of sum f(x) delta x as the integral of f(x).
- constant-of-integration-from-point — Find the constant of integration from a point: After integrating, substitute a known point (or initial condition) to find c.
- standard-integrals — Use standard integrals (e^(kx), 1/x, trig): Integrate e^(kx), 1/(ax + b), sin(kx), cos(kx) and sec^2(kx) directly, remembering the 1/k factor.
- reverse-chain-rule — Integrate by reverse chain rule (recognition): Recognise integrands of the form f'(x)[f(x)]^n or (ax + b)^n and integrate by inspection.

### areas-by-integration
- area-under-curve — Find the area under a curve: Integrate between the x-intercepts or given limits; where the curve is below the x-axis the integral is negative, so split at roots and use magnitudes.
- area-between-curve-and-line — Find the area between a curve and a line: Integrate (top - bottom), or combine the area under the curve with triangle/trapezium areas.
- area-under-parametric-curve — Find the area under a parametric curve: Use Area = integral of y (dx/dt) dt with the limits changed to parameter values.

### integration-techniques
- integration-by-parts — Integrate by parts: Choose u (often ln x or a polynomial) and dv/dx, apply the formula (twice if needed) and simplify.
- integration-by-substitution — Integrate by substitution: Use the given substitution to rewrite the whole integral (including dx) in terms of the new variable, then integrate.
- change-limits-in-substitution — Change the limits when substituting: Convert x-limits into limits for the new variable (u or theta) so you never need to substitute back.
- integrate-using-trig-identities — Use trig identities to integrate: Rewrite sin^2, cos^2 or products using double angle identities before integrating.
- integrate-partial-fractions-to-logs — Integrate partial fractions to logarithms: Integrate A/(ax + b) to (A/a) ln|ax + b| for each fraction, then combine logs into a single exact form.

### partial-fractions
- algebraic-division-improper — Divide out an improper algebraic fraction: When the numerator degree >= denominator degree, divide first to get a polynomial plus a proper fraction (e.g. 4 - 7/(2x + 3)).
- partial-fractions — Split a fraction into partial fractions: Write A/(ax + b) + B/(cx + d) (or with a repeated factor B/(ax + b)^2), multiply through, and find the constants by substitution or comparing coefficients.

### differential-equations
- form-differential-equation — Form a differential equation from a description: Translate "rate of change is proportional to..." into dy/dt = k(...) with the correct sign.
- separate-variables-and-integrate — Separate the variables and integrate: Rearrange to f(y) dy = g(x) dx, integrate both sides and include one constant of integration.
- find-constants-from-conditions — Use initial/boundary conditions to find constants: Substitute given (t, y) values to find the constant of integration and any proportionality constant.
- rearrange-solution-explicitly — Rearrange a DE solution into the required form: Remove logs (exponentiate) and make the dependent variable the subject, e.g. V = A/(e^(-kt) + B) or h = A + Be^(-kt).

### numerical-methods
- change-of-sign-root-location — Show a root lies in an interval (change of sign): Evaluate f at both ends, show a sign change, state f is continuous, and conclude; use a tight interval to confirm a root to given accuracy.
- fixed-point-iteration — Use an iteration formula: Substitute repeatedly into x_(n+1) = g(x_n), giving values to the required accuracy.
- iteration-diagrams-and-convergence — Draw cobweb/staircase diagrams and judge convergence: Draw a cobweb or staircase from x_1 and decide whether (and to which root) the iteration converges.
- newton-raphson — Apply the Newton-Raphson method: Use x_(n+1) = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n) and know when it fails (e.g. f'(x_n) = 0 at a stationary point).
- rearrange-to-iterative-form — Rearrange an equation into a given iterative form: Manipulate f(x) = 0 or f'(x) = 0 algebraically into x = g(x) exactly as printed.
- trapezium-rule — Estimate an integral with the trapezium rule: Use (h/2)[y0 + yn + 2(y1 + ... + y(n-1))] with the correct strip width h.
- trapezium-accuracy-and-reuse — Judge or reuse a trapezium estimate: Explain over/underestimates from the curve shape, comment on accuracy, or scale a previous estimate using log laws or constant factors.

### parametric-equations
- parametric-to-cartesian — Convert parametric equations to Cartesian form: Eliminate the parameter by rearranging or by using a trig identity (sin^2 + cos^2 = 1, cos 2t = 1 - 2 sin^2 t, sec^2 = 1 + tan^2).
- parametric-domain-and-endpoints — Use the parameter range to find domain restrictions and endpoints: Use the allowed values of t to find the end points of the curve and the restricted x or y values.

### vectors
- vector-between-points — Find the vector between two points: Use AB = OB - OA (or add vectors along a path) in i, j, k form.
- vector-magnitude — Find the magnitude of a vector: Use |ai + bj + ck| = sqrt(a^2 + b^2 + c^2) for lengths and distances.
- parallel-and-collinear-vectors — Use parallel and collinear vectors: Vectors are parallel if one is a scalar multiple of the other; collinear points share a parallel vector and a point.
- vector-ratios-and-geometric-proof — Use vector ratios to locate points and prove geometric results: Write a position vector using a ratio or scalar lambda, compare coefficients of non-parallel vectors, and prove ratios or shapes.

### proof
- proof-by-contradiction — Prove by contradiction: Assume the negation, reason algebraically (e.g. factorise and check every integer factor pair) to something impossible, and conclude the original statement.
- algebraic-proof-odd-even-multiples — Prove results using general odd/even/multiple forms: Represent numbers as 2k, 2k + 1, 3k + 1, etc., expand, and factor to show the required form.
- prove-inequality-or-positivity — Prove an inequality or that an expression is always positive: Complete the square (square >= 0), or manipulate an inequality carefully, reversing the sign when multiplying/dividing by a negative.
- proof-by-exhaustion-or-counterexample — Prove by exhaustion or disprove by counterexample: Split into a complete set of cases (or check every value) and show the result in each; or disprove a claim with one specific counterexample.

### exam-technique
- show-that-given-answer — Present a full "show that" argument: When the answer is printed, show every step (including key intermediate lines) with no errors, and finish at exactly the given result.
- use-hence-previous-result — Use a previous part ("hence"): Spot how an earlier result (identity, factor, expansion, derivative) transforms the new problem, and use it explicitly.
- reject-invalid-solutions — Reject solutions that are invalid: Check each solution against the domain, the context or the original equation (log of a negative, negative length, |r| >= 1, outside the interval) and reject with a reason.
- exact-form-answers — Give exact (calculator-free) answers: Keep surds, pi, e and ln in exact form and combine logs, when told to show working or give an exact answer.
- set-notation-answers — Write answers in set notation: Express ranges of values using correct set notation, including unions and intersections of intervals.
- accuracy-units-and-rounding — Give answers to the required accuracy and units: Round only at the end to the stated accuracy (s.f., d.p., nearest minute/metre) and include units; convert time formats.
- explain-with-reason — Explain or justify with a reason and conclusion: Give a clear mathematical reason plus a minimal conclusion when asked to explain, justify or give a reason.

## Question types (id — definition)
- optimisation-constrained-shape — A solid or container with a fixed volume: show a surface-area formula in one variable, then use calculus to find and justify the optimum.
- optimisation-modelled-quantity — A context model (speed, growth rate, bounce height) whose maximum is found by differentiating the model itself, often needing a product/chain rule and interpretation.
- stationary-points-and-nature — Differentiate a given curve (once or twice) to find or verify stationary points, classify them, or find where the curve is concave/convex or has an inflection.
- differentiate-to-given-form — Quotient/product/chain rule on a single function to reach a printed form, often followed by increasing/decreasing or a condition on a constant.
- first-principles — Prove a derivative (polynomial or sin/cos) from the gradient of a chord and a limit as h -> 0.
- implicit-differentiation-tangents — Find dy/dx for an implicitly defined curve (or x = f(y)), then use it for a normal, tangent, extreme point or inflection.
- turning-point-by-iteration — Differentiate, set the derivative to zero, rearrange into x = g(x), then iterate and/or confirm the root with a sign change.
- locate-root-and-newton-raphson — Show a root lies in an interval by sign change, then apply Newton-Raphson or judge an iteration from a cobweb diagram.
- trapezium-rule-estimate — Apply the trapezium rule to a table of values, then reuse, judge or compare the estimate (sometimes with the exact integral).
- exponential-model-in-context — Find the constants of an exponential model from data, then predict, differentiate for a rate, compare two models or evaluate/adapt the model.
- log-linear-graph-model — A straight-line graph of log y against x or log x is used to find the constants in y = ab^x or y = kx^n, then interpret or use the model.
- log-laws-and-equations — Manipulate or solve equations using the laws of logarithms, usually removing logs and rejecting invalid roots.
- exponential-equations — Solve an equation with the unknown in the power (by a common base, logs, or a hidden quadratic), sometimes with a sketch of y = a^x.
- find-curve-from-gradient — Integrate powers of x (indefinite), using a point, turning point or factor to find the constant(s) of integration.
- area-between-curve-and-line — Algebraic integration of polynomial/fractional powers to find an exact area bounded by a curve, the axis and/or a tangent or straight line.
- definite-integrals-standard-forms — A short definite integral of a standard form (1/(ax + b), (ax + b)^-2, x^(1/2)), sometimes written as the limit of a sum, simplified exactly.
- integration-by-parts — An exact definite integral or area needing integration by parts (possibly twice or combined with a normal).
- integration-by-substitution — Use a given substitution (including trigonometric) to transform and evaluate an integral exactly, changing the limits.
- partial-fractions-then-calculus — Express a rational function in partial fractions (or divide an improper fraction), then integrate exactly or differentiate.
- parametric-area — Set up the integral of y(dx/dt) dt for a parametric region, simplify with trig identities and evaluate exactly.
- de-formulate-from-rates — A worded rate statement (proportional to..., constant volume rate, flow in/out) is turned into a DE, sometimes via connected rates, then solved and interpreted.
- de-given-separable — The DE is given; separate variables (possibly after partial fractions or a substitution), use conditions, rearrange and interpret.
- binomial-expansion-and-approximation — Expand (a + bx)^n, state validity and use it to approximate a value, sometimes combined with partial fractions or integration.
- vectors-lengths-and-shapes — Use magnitudes of vectors (Pythagoras in 3D) for lengths, distances, areas or angles, often to classify or measure a shape.
- vectors-parallel-and-ratios — Use scalar multiples to show parallel/collinear results or to locate a point dividing a line in a ratio.
- proof-by-contradiction — Prove a statement by assuming its negation (parity, factor pairs, trig inequality) and reaching a contradiction.
- proof-by-cases-and-counterexample — Prove a number property by writing n in general forms (2k, 2k+1, 3k+r) and checking every case, or disprove a claim with a counterexample.
- circle-line-tangency — Find a circle's centre/radius or equation, then substitute a line and use the discriminant (or the tangent-radius property) for tangents or two intersections.
- circle-centre-radius-and-distances — Complete the square for the centre and radius, then use distances from the centre (furthest point, two circles intersecting).
- small-angle-approximations — Replace sin, cos and tan by their small-angle approximations to estimate a value, a root or an expression.
- radians-sectors-and-regions — Use radian arc-length and sector-area formulae (with triangles/segments) to find angles, lengths, areas or perimeters.
- trig-identity-then-solve — Prove or use an identity (double/compound angle, reciprocal, Pythagorean) and then solve a related trig equation in an interval.
- harmonic-form-and-trig-models — Write a cos x + b sin x as R cos(x +- alpha) and/or use a sinusoidal model to find maximum/minimum values and the times they occur.
- parametric-differentiation-tangents — Find dy/dx parametrically and use it for a tangent or gradient at a point (sometimes after converting to Cartesian form).
- parametric-to-cartesian-and-intersections — Convert parametric equations to Cartesian form (usually by a trig identity), then use the parameter range and/or find intersections or a range of k.
- factor-theorem-find-constant — Given a linear factor of a polynomial with an unknown coefficient, set f(a) = 0 and solve for the constant.
- polynomial-factorise-and-solve — Use a known root/factor to factorise a cubic, then solve exactly, find intersections, or count real roots of a related equation.
- quadratic-and-polynomial-graphs — Complete the square or use roots/turning points to sketch a quadratic or cubic, read off features, or define a region with inequalities.
- linear-and-quadratic-models — Fit a linear or quadratic model to a context (trajectory, cost, growth) from given conditions, then use it, evaluate it or state a limitation.
- composite-and-inverse-functions — Evaluate or form composite functions, find inverses with their domains, and state ranges.
- modulus-and-transformations — Work with y = a|x - b| + c or |f(x)|: find the vertex, solve modulus equations/inequalities, find k for intersections, or map points under transformations.
- ap-gp-in-context — Use nth-term and sum formulae for an AP or GP (often a savings, speed, profit or race context), including forming and solving a quadratic in n.
- geometric-series-with-unknowns — Terms or sums of a GP are given in terms of an unknown; form and solve an equation (ratio property or sum formula), then use S_infinity.
- sigma-notation-sums — Evaluate a sigma-notation sum by splitting into AP/GP parts, adjusting the starting index, or telescoping.
- recurrence-and-periodic-sequences — Generate terms from u_(n+1) = f(u_n), find an unknown constant, and/or sum a periodic sequence.
